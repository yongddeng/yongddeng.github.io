# Stochastic Process
---
A stochastic process is a random function of time, or equally a family of random variables indexed by time, and its finite-dimensional distributions join the two views. This note fixes the objects that martingales and Markov chains are built on, the law of a process, its stationarity and ergodicity, its increments, and the filtrations and stopping times that record what is known when. Billingsley's [Probability and Measure]() and Doob's [Stochastic Processes]() are the references<!-- , and a process is a vast object that takes time to comprehend -->.


## I
---
**Definition.** Let $T$ be a totally ordered set of times, usually $\mathbb{N}$ or $[0, \infty)$, $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ a probability space and $(\mathbb{S}, \mathcal{S})$ a measurable [state space](). A [stochastic process]() $X = (X\_t)\_{t \in T}$ is a family of $\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{S}$-measurable maps $X\_t \colon \Omega \to \mathbb{S}$, its [sample path]() at $\omega$ is the function $t \mapsto X\_t(\omega)$, and its [finite-dimensional distributions]() are the laws

$$
\mu_{t_1, \dots, t_n}(B) = P\big((X_{t_1}, \dots, X_{t_n}) \in B\big), \qquad B \in \mathcal{S}^{\otimes n},
$$

over all $n$ and all $t\_1 < \dots < t\_n$ in $T$.

*Remarks.* The same object is a function $X \colon T \times \Omega \to \mathbb{S}$, written $X\_t(\omega)$ or $X(t, \omega)$, and a single random element of the path space $\mathbb{S}^T$, whose product $\sigma$-algebra is generated by the cylinders $\lbrace X\_{t\_1} \in B\_1, \dots, X\_{t\_n} \in B\_n \rbrace$. These form a $\pi$-system, so the finite-dimensional distributions determine the law of $X$. For $\mathbb{S} = \mathbb{R}$ each is fixed by its joint distribution function, and it is a product measure exactly when $X\_{t\_1}, \dots, X\_{t\_n}$ are independent.

**Theorem** ([Kolmogorov extension]())**.** Let $\mathbb{S}$ be a complete separable metric space with its Borel sets, such as $\mathbb{R}$. A family of probability measures $\mu\_{t\_1, \dots, t\_n}$ on $\mathbb{S}^n$ that is consistent, in that deleting any $t\_k$ projects $\mu\_{t\_1, \dots, t\_n}$ onto the measure of the remaining times, is the family of finite-dimensional distributions of a process on $\Omega = \mathbb{S}^T$, and its law on the product $\sigma$-algebra is unique.

<details><summary>Proof</summary>
Consistency makes $P(\lbrace (x_{t_1}, \dots, x_{t_n}) \in B \rbrace) = \mu_{t_1, \dots, t_n}(B)$ a well-defined, finitely additive measure on the algebra of cylinders. For countable additivity let cylinders $C_m$ decrease with $P(C_m) \geq \varepsilon$. Inner regularity on a complete separable space gives cylinders $K_m \subseteq C_m$ with compact bases and $P(C_m \setminus K_m) < 2^{-m-1}\varepsilon$, so $P(K_1 \cap \dots \cap K_m) \geq \varepsilon/2$, and a diagonal argument over the countably many coordinates involved finds a point in every $K_m$. Carathéodory's theorem extends $P$, and uniqueness is the $\pi$-system argument. &#8718;
</details>

*Remark.* Each event of the product $\sigma$-algebra depends on countably many coordinates, so for $T = [0, \infty)$ the set of continuous paths is not even an event there. Continuous-time processes are therefore built as a continuous or càdlàg [modification](), with $\tilde{X}\_t = X\_t$ almost surely for each $t$, which Kolmogorov's continuity criterion provides when $\operatorname{E}\lvert X\_t - X\_s \rvert^\alpha \leq C\lvert t - s \rvert^{1+\beta}$ for some $\alpha, \beta > 0$. Time and state may each be discrete or continuous, which gives four kinds of process.


## II
---
**Definition.** A process $X$ is

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
\text{strict-sense stationary} & \text{if } (X_{t_1 + h}, \dots, X_{t_n + h}) \overset{d}{=} (X_{t_1}, \dots, X_{t_n}) \text{ for all } n, t_1, \dots, t_n \text{ and } h, \\
\text{stationary of order } N & \text{if the same holds for every } n \leq N, \\
\text{wide-sense stationary} & \text{if } X_t \in L^2, \ \operatorname{E}X_t = \mu \text{ and } \operatorname{Cov}(X_{t+h}, X_t) = \gamma(h) \text{ for all } t \text{ and } h,
\end{array}
$$

where $\gamma$ is the [autocovariance]() and $\rho(h) = \gamma(h)/\gamma(0)$ the [autocorrelation]().

**Proposition.** (i) Stationarity of order $N$ implies stationarity of every order $M < N$; (ii) a [strict-sense stationary]() process in $L^2$ is [wide-sense stationary](), but not conversely; (iii) a Gaussian process, one whose finite-dimensional distributions are all normal, is strict-sense stationary if and only if it is wide-sense stationary.

<details><summary>Proof</summary>
(i) Marginalise the shifted $N$-dimensional law. (ii) The mean and the covariance are functionals of the one- and two-dimensional laws, whereas independent $X_t$ alternating between $N(0, 1)$ and the uniform law on $(-\sqrt{3}, \sqrt{3})$ share mean $0$ and variance $1$ and are uncorrelated without being identically distributed. (iii) A normal law is fixed by its mean vector and covariance matrix, which wide-sense stationarity leaves unchanged under shifts. &#8718;
</details>

**Examples.** [White noise](), uncorrelated with mean $0$ and variance $\sigma^2$, is wide-sense stationary with $\gamma(h) = \sigma^2 I\_{\lbrace h = 0 \rbrace}$, and strict-sense stationary when it is i.i.d. The [autoregression]() $X\_t = \phi X\_{t-1} + \varepsilon\_t$ with $\lvert \phi \rvert < 1$, $X\_0 \sim N(0, 1)$ and independent $\varepsilon\_t \sim N(0, 1 - \phi^2)$ is Gaussian with mean $0$ and $\gamma(h) = \phi^{\lvert h \rvert}$, so it is strict-sense stationary.

**Definition.** For a wide-sense stationary sequence, the [time average]() $\bar{X}\_n = n^{-1}\sum\_{t=0}^{n-1} X\_t$ of one path estimates the [ensemble average]() $\mu = \operatorname{E}X\_t$ over all paths at one time. The sequence is [mean-ergodic]() if $\bar{X}\_n \to \mu$ in $L^2$, [autocovariance-ergodic]() if $n^{-1}\sum\_{t=0}^{n-1}(X\_{t+h} - \mu)(X\_t - \mu) \to \gamma(h)$ in $L^2$ for every $h$, and [wide-sense ergodic]() if it is both.

**Theorem** (mean ergodicity)**.** A wide-sense stationary sequence is mean-ergodic if and only if $n^{-1}\sum\_{k=0}^{n-1}\gamma(k) \to 0$, in particular whenever $\gamma(k) \to 0$.

<details><summary>Proof</summary>
Since $n^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\gamma(k) = \operatorname{Cov}(\bar{X}_n, X_0)$, the Cauchy-Schwarz bound $\operatorname{Cov}(\bar{X}_n, X_0)^2 \leq \gamma(0)\operatorname{Var}\bar{X}_n$ gives necessity. Conversely $\operatorname{Var}\bar{X}_n = n^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\operatorname{Cov}(X_j, \bar{X}_n)$, where $n\operatorname{Cov}(X_j, \bar{X}_n) = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\gamma(k - j) = C(n - j) + C(j + 1) - \gamma(0)$ with $C(m) = \sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\gamma(k)$. As $C(m) = o(m)$ and $\lvert C(m) \rvert \leq m\gamma(0)$, the sum over $j$ is $o(n^2)$, so $\operatorname{Var}\bar{X}_n \to 0$. &#8718;
</details>

*Remarks.* Ergodicity lets one long path stand in for many paths at a fixed time, which is often all there is to observe. Neither kind of stationarity implies it, as the random level $X\_t = Z + \varepsilon\_t$, with $Z$ independent of an i.i.d. noise, is strict-sense stationary with $\gamma(k) = \operatorname{Var}Z$ for every $k \neq 0$, and its time average tends to $Z$ rather than to $\mu$. For a strict-sense stationary sequence in $L^1$, [Birkhoff's ergodic theorem]() gives $\bar{X}\_n \to \operatorname{E}(X\_0 \mid \mathcal{I})$ almost surely, with $\mathcal{I}$ the shift-invariant events, and the limit is $\mu$ when $\mathcal{I}$ is trivial<!-- , and in continuous time the sums become integrals $T^{-1}\int\_0^T X\_t \, \mathrm{d}t$ -->.

## III
---
**Definition.** A process with $X\_0 = 0$ has [independent increments]() if $X\_t - X\_s$ is independent of $\sigma(X\_r \mid r \leq s)$ for all $s \leq t$, so that increments over disjoint intervals are independent, and [stationary increments]() if $X\_t - X\_s \overset{d}{=} X\_{t-s}$ for all $s \leq t$.

**Proposition.** Let $X\_0 = 0$. (i) For $T = \mathbb{N}$, $X$ has independent and stationary increments if and only if $X\_n = U\_1 + \dots + U\_n$ for i.i.d. $U\_k$, and then $(X\_1, \dots, X\_n)$ has density $f\_U(x\_1)f\_U(x\_2 - x\_1) \cdots f\_U(x\_n - x\_{n-1})$ when $U\_1$ has density $f\_U$; (ii) if such a process lies in $L^2$, in discrete time or in continuous time with $t \mapsto \operatorname{E}X\_t$ measurable, then $\operatorname{E}X\_t = \mu t$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(X\_s, X\_t) = \sigma^2 \min(s, t)$ for some $\mu \in \mathbb{R}$ and $\sigma \geq 0$.

<details><summary>Proof</summary>
(i) The increments $U_k = X_k - X_{k-1}$ are independent and identically distributed exactly under the two hypotheses, and the density follows by the change of variables $u_k = x_k - x_{k-1}$, whose Jacobian is $1$. (ii) Independent stationary increments make $m(t) = \operatorname{E}X_t$ and $v(t) = \operatorname{Var}X_t$ additive, $m(s + t) = m(s) + m(t)$, and $v \geq 0$ is then monotone, while measurable or monotone solutions of Cauchy's functional equation are linear. For $s \leq t$, $\operatorname{Cov}(X_s, X_t) = \operatorname{Var}X_s + \operatorname{Cov}(X_s, X_t - X_s) = \sigma^2 s$. &#8718;
</details>

**Examples** (partial sums)**.** With $X\_0 = 0$,

- the [simple random walk]() $S\_n = \xi\_1 + \dots + \xi\_n$ with i.i.d. steps $P(\xi\_k = 1) = p$ and $P(\xi\_k = -1) = q = 1 - p$ has $\operatorname{E}S\_n = n(2p - 1)$ and $\operatorname{Var}S\_n = 4npq$, and $(S\_n + n)/2 \sim \operatorname{Bin}(n, p)$ counts its up-steps, so its increments are stationary while $S\_n$ itself is not;
- the [Poisson process]() $N\_t = \max \lbrace n \mid \tau\_1 + \dots + \tau\_n \leq t \rbrace$ built on i.i.d. waiting times $\tau\_k \sim \operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)$ has independent increments $N\_t - N\_s \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda(t - s))$, so $\operatorname{E}N\_t = \operatorname{Var}N\_t = \lambda t$, and its paths are non-decreasing right-continuous steps of height $1$.

*Remarks.* The symmetric walk, $p = 1/2$, visits every integer infinitely often almost surely, whereas for $p \neq 1/2$ the strong law gives $S\_n/n \to 2p - 1$ and the walk drifts away for good. Pearson named the random walk in 1905. In continuous time the processes with independent stationary increments and càdlàg paths are the [Lévy processes](), Brownian motion and the Poisson process among them.

*Remark.* Centred complex random variables in $L^2$ form a Hilbert space under $\langle X, Y \rangle = \operatorname{E}(X\bar{Y})$, once almost surely equal ones are identified, so a centred process in $L^2$ is a curve $t \mapsto X\_t$ in it. Linear prediction of $X\_t$ from the past up to $u$ is then orthogonal projection onto the closed span of $\lbrace X\_s \mid s \leq u \rbrace$.

*Remark.* Regression between time series inherits the assumptions of ordinary least squares only for stationary series, and two independent random walks regressed on each other look significantly related, a [spurious regression]() (Granger and Newbold, 1974). In practice a log transform stabilises a variance that grows with the level, differencing removes a trend or a unit root, the sample acf and pacf show the dependence that remains, and [STL decomposition]() splits $y\_t = S\_t + T\_t + R\_t$ into seasonal, trend and remainder parts, or $\log y\_t$ when they multiply.


## IV
---
**Definition.** A [filtration]() $\mathbb{F} = (\mathcal{F}\_t)\_{t \in T}$ is an increasing family of sub-$\sigma$-algebras of $\mathcal{F}$, and $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{F}, P)$ is a [filtered probability space](). A process $X$ is [adapted]() if every $X\_t$ is $\mathcal{F}\_t$-measurable, and, in discrete time, [predictable]() if every $X\_t$ is $\mathcal{F}\_{t-1}$-measurable. The [natural filtration]() $\mathcal{F}^X\_t = \sigma(X\_s \mid s \leq t)$ is the smallest to which $X$ is adapted, and $\mathbb{F}$ is [coarser]() than $\mathbb{G}$, written $\mathbb{F} \preceq \mathbb{G}$, if $\mathcal{F}\_t \subseteq \mathcal{G}\_t$ for every $t$.

**Definition.** A random time $\tau \colon \Omega \to T \cup \lbrace \infty \rbrace$ is a [stopping time]() if $\lbrace \tau \leq t \rbrace \in \mathcal{F}\_t$ for every $t$, and the [stopped $\sigma$-algebra]() $\mathcal{F}\_\tau = \lbrace A \in \mathcal{F}\_\infty \mid A \cap \lbrace \tau \leq t \rbrace \in \mathcal{F}\_t \text{ for every } t \rbrace$, with $\mathcal{F}\_\infty = \sigma(\bigcup\_t \mathcal{F}\_t)$, holds the events decided by time $\tau$.

**Proposition.** For stopping times $\tau\_1$ and $\tau\_2$ of $\mathbb{F}$, (i) a random time $\tau$ is a stopping time if and only if $\lbrace \tau > t \rbrace \in \mathcal{F}\_t$ for every $t$, and in discrete time if and only if $\lbrace \tau = t \rbrace \in \mathcal{F}\_t$ for every $t$; (ii) $\tau\_1$ is a stopping time of every finer filtration; (iii) $\tau\_1 \vee \tau\_2$ and $\tau\_1 \wedge \tau\_2$ are stopping times, and so is $\tau\_1 + \tau\_2$ in discrete time.

<details><summary>Proof</summary>
(i) $\lbrace \tau > t \rbrace$ is the complement of $\lbrace \tau \leq t \rbrace$, and in discrete time $\lbrace \tau = t \rbrace = \lbrace \tau \leq t \rbrace \setminus \lbrace \tau \leq t - 1 \rbrace$ while $\lbrace \tau \leq t \rbrace = \bigcup_{s \leq t}\lbrace \tau = s \rbrace$. (ii) $\mathcal{F}_t \subseteq \mathcal{G}_t$. (iii) $\lbrace \tau_1 \vee \tau_2 \leq t \rbrace$ and $\lbrace \tau_1 \wedge \tau_2 \leq t \rbrace$ are the intersection and the union of $\lbrace \tau_1 \leq t \rbrace$ and $\lbrace \tau_2 \leq t \rbrace$, and $\lbrace \tau_1 + \tau_2 = n \rbrace = \bigcup_{k=0}^{n}\lbrace \tau_1 = k \rbrace \cap \lbrace \tau_2 = n - k \rbrace$. &#8718;
</details>

**Examples.** For a process $X$ adapted to $\mathbb{F}$,

- a constant time is a stopping time of every filtration, and the only kind for the trivial filtration $\mathcal{F}\_t = \lbrace \emptyset, \Omega \rbrace$, whereas for $\mathcal{F}\_t = \mathcal{F}$ every random time is one;
- the [first hitting time]() $\tau\_B = \inf \lbrace t \mid X\_t \in B \rbrace$ of a Borel set $B$ in discrete time is a stopping time, as $\lbrace \tau\_B = t \rbrace = \bigcap\_{s < t}\lbrace X\_s \notin B \rbrace \cap \lbrace X\_t \in B \rbrace \in \mathcal{F}\_t$;
- the [last exit time]() $\sup \lbrace t \leq N \mid X\_t \in B \rbrace$ is in general not, since whether it equals $t$ depends on the path after $t$.

*Remark.* A stopping time is a rule that decides whether to stop at $t$ from what is known at $t$, without looking ahead. In continuous time hitting times need more, a closed $B$ and continuous paths, or else the [usual conditions](), a complete right-continuous filtration, under which the début theorem makes $\tau\_B$ a stopping time for every Borel $B$ and progressively measurable $X$.
